Wheel Selector

Find the right rim width, depth and weight for your bike, terrain and power

Choosing wheels usually starts with a spec sheet and ends with a guess. Rim width, rim depth and wheelset weight all trade off against each other, and the right balance depends on your tyre size, your terrain, and how much power you actually produce — not just what's fastest on paper.

This calculator works through that trade-off the way we'd work through it for a coaching client: starting from your tyres and riding conditions, checking what's compatible, then weighing aero gain against weight penalty using your FTP and rider weight.

Enter your surface type, tyre sizes, FTP, rider weight and terrain below, and it will return:

  • A recommended internal rim width range, checked against your tyres for ETRTO compatibility
  • A recommended rim depth range, weighing aero gain against weight penalty for your terrain and power
  • A recommended wheelset weight range
  • Your tyres' expected measured width once mounted, so you can check it against your own frame clearance
  • A shortlist of carbon wheelsets scored against your specific profile

How rim width and tyre size actually interact

The internal width of a rim changes how a tyre measures once it's mounted — a 40mm-labelled tyre can measure several millimetres wider on a 25mm internal rim than it does on a 19mm rim. That's why "what rim width for a 40mm gravel tyre" or "what internal rim width for 700x28" doesn't have one universal answer — it depends on the specific rim.

The ETRTO standard defines which tyre and rim widths are compatible, and this calculator checks your tyre sizes against it directly. If you run different tyres for training and racing, or split time between road and gravel, the calculator checks all of them together — a rim that's compatible with one tyre but not another isn't a real recommendation, just a partial one.

Rim depth: aero gain versus weight penalty

Rim depth is where most buying guides default to "deeper is faster," which is only true in specific conditions. Aero benefit from a deeper rim scales with speed, which means it scales with your absolute power output more than your power-to-weight ratio. A rider producing 300 watts on flat terrain gets more from a deep rim than a lighter rider producing fewer absolute watts, even if the lighter rider has the better watts-per-kilo.

On climbing terrain, the maths flips. Rotating weight matters more than aerodynamics once gradient dominates the ride, and that's a power-to-weight question, not an absolute-power one. This calculator uses both numbers — separating where each one actually applies, rather than blending them into one vague "rider strength" score.

Wide tyres change this further. Once a tyre measures wider than the rim it's mounted to, the airflow transition at the rim-to-tyre junction is compromised, and no amount of extra rim depth fully corrects for it — though depth still contributes aero benefit on its own, just less than the same rim would return under a narrower tyre. This is a common blind spot in gravel wheel selection, where riders chase 50mm-plus rim depth on tyres already wider than any production rim.

Built for road, gravel and mixed riders

Whether you're choosing a gravel wheelset, a road bike aero wheelset, or a single wheelset to cover both, the calculator adjusts its logic to the surface type you select. Mixed-surface riders get an additional check: because road and gravel tyres often sit at opposite ends of the width range, the tool flags when there's no single rim width that properly suits both, rather than quietly picking one and ignoring the other.

A note on the recommendations

This tool gives sizing guidance and a shortlist of wheelsets that fit that guidance — it's a starting point for narrowing a wheel search, not a substitute for checking manufacturer specifications, frame clearance, or hookless rim pressure limits before you buy. Specs shown are manufacturer-claimed at time of publishing. Fitment and safety remain the rider's responsibility.
